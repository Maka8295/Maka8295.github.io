Herein are set down the boards of my own hand: keyboards that sense a key's travel by magnetism, small tools for reading the memory of other machines, a badge for a gathering of hackers, and a venerable games console redrawn on four layers of copper. Every board was drawn in KiCad, and every three-dimensional view on these pages was rendered straight from its board file. Where a work was made for my employer and may not be shared, it is marked with a seal.

Chapter I

Lucca 58HE

Of a keyboard that feels the depth of every keystroke

A fifty-eight key split keyboard in which no key is a switch at all. Beneath each cap sits a magnet, and beneath the board a DRV5053 hall sensor, read through an ADG732 thirty-two channel multiplexer into the analogue inputs of an RP2040. The travel of a key is resolved to about seven micrometres, which makes rapid trigger and an actuation point anywhere from nought to three and a half millimetres a matter of firmware alone.

The first halves were brought up under KMK and CircuitPython. The 58HE is now the author's daily keyboard; this very page was typed upon it.
Controller
Waveshare RP2040-Zero, one per half
Sensing
DRV5053 hall sensors via ADG732 multiplexer
Lighting
SK6803 Mini-E per-key RGB, ten-point underglow
Link
USB-C; SPI or UART between the halves
Sundries
SSD1306 OLED, three-dimensionally printed case

Chapter II

FT2232H Breakout

Of a bridge between a computer and the wires of other machines

Two channels of FTDI's FT2232H, each passed through an SN74LVC8T245 level shifter so that the far side may speak at 1.8, 2.5, 3.3 or 5 volts at the flick of a DIP switch. A hardware isolation switch lets a chip clip be detached from its target without pulling a single wire, and switchable 4.7 kΩ pull-ups make I2C work on either channel. JTAG, SPI, I2C and UART are all within its gift.

FT2232HL, USB-C
Shifting
Two SN74LVC8T245, selectable target voltage
Power
LDK130 at 1.8 and 2.5 V, AP2112K at 3.3 V
Protection
USBLC6-2 on the USB lines

Chapter III

Flumper

Of a flash dumper, and the three editions through which it passed

Flumper is a USB tool for reading the flash and EEPROM memories of other devices, built for KAGE Engineering. The first edition carried an FT232H and taught hard lessons about USB high speed: the TVS diodes were too capacitive, the differential pair was sloppy, and a misnamed net left the multiplexers without power. The second edition moved to the dual-channel FT2232H, added level shifting from 1.8 to 5 volts and sixteen spare GPIO, and matched the USB pair to 90 ohms over an unbroken ground. The third, shown above, shrinks onto an FT4222H and pushes the target memory out onto a socketed cartridge, so a burned chip can be swapped rather than mourned.

The cartridge, memcart, carries the chip and three solder jumpers; a ribbon adapter, flumpcable, reaches a memory footprint still soldered to its board.

0.1 FT232H · 0.2 FT2232H · 0.3 FT4222HQ
Shifting
TMUX1574 switches, 1.8 to 5 V targets
Targets
SPI flash, I2C EEPROM, via socketed cartridge
Power
LDK130 at 1.8 V, AP2112K at 3.3 V

Chapter IV

Badge 2026

Of a badge made for TENGUCON 3.0, Akihabara

A wearable computer for a Tokyo hacker gathering. An ESP32-S3 module drives a display over SPI, a Ra-01S LoRa radio for chatting badge to badge, a microSD card on the SDMMC bus, and an infrared blaster with a TSOP38238 receiver for learning and replaying remote-control codes. Below the screen lies a fifty-eight key matrix of low-profile tactile switches, so the badge is also a keyboard, and ten addressable LEDs light its edges.

The firmware is written in Rust on ESP-IDF, and carries a LoRa chat, an IR blaster that reads Flipper Zero files, an SD card explorer, and a loader for ELF apps built against its SDK.

Chapter V

Shuriken 2

Of a controller that governs the power and ports of devices under test

The second Shuriken, a multifunction controller for KAGE's test benches. An RP2040 commands a USB2514B four-port hub, so a single cable from the host fans out to four USB-C ports, each with its own power switch. Barrel-jack inputs feed TPS56A37 buck converters, and AO4407A high-side switches, polyfuses and TVS diodes let the board cycle power to a device under test without a hand ever reaching for the plug.

Its forebear, the first Shuriken, was built around an STM32G474 with CAN, RS485 and relays. The second trims that to what the benches actually used.

RP2040 with W25Q128 flash
Hub
Microchip USB2514B, four USB-C ports
Power
TPS56A37 buck regulators, AO4407A switched outputs
Protection

microSD slot

Chapter VI

Lucca58

Of the same keyboard, with ordinary switches

The mechanical sibling of the 58HE: the same fifty-eight key split footprint, but with hot-swap sockets for ordinary switches in place of hall sensors, which makes it a far simpler and cheaper board to build. Two layouts were drawn, one columnar and one row-staggered, and both carry production files ready for JLCPCB.

Chapter VII

Lazy Kiwi

Of a small wireless brain for other projects

A compact ESP32-S3 board with its antenna etched into the copper, external PSRAM and flash, and a USB-C port wired straight to the chip's native USB. Boot and reset buttons sit on the board, and the whole is meant to drop into larger projects as their wireless part.

ESP32-S3 with PCB antenna
Memory
ESP-PSRAM64H (8 MB), W25Q128 flash (16 MB)
USB
USB-C with USBLC6-2 protection

Chapter VIII

SFFSFC

Of the Super Famicom, redrawn

A small-form-factor re-engineering of the Super Famicom's second revision motherboard. The original two layers are being redrawn on four, with HDMI video and TOSLINK audio brought out directly, so that a stock chipset may drive a modern display without any scaler between. The work stands at schematic capture.

Target
SFC / SNES revision 2 chipset
Stackup
Four layers
Outputs
HDMI video, TOSLINK audio
